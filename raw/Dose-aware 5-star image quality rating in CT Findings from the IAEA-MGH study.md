## 1\. Introduction

The exponential global volume of CT imaging has been paralleled by an increasing clinical demand for measurable image quality and responsible radiation use. However, unlike laboratory image analysis, there is no internationally harmonized method for radiologists to score diagnostic CT image quality in routine practice. The absence of standardization in image quality assessment carries measurable consequences, including unwarranted radiation exposure from repeat imaging, loss of diagnostic confidence, sub-optimal protocol design, and missed opportunities for systematic dose reduction. Objective physical metrics of CT image quality, such as noise, contrast-to-noise ratio, and spatial resolution, are well established, but they do not directly reflect clinical diagnostic acceptability. Consequently, subjective assessment methods, often based on Likert-type scales, remain essential in routine practice. There have been many attempts to achieve subjective assessment of image quality, mostly based on the Likert scale.

Among the available dose descriptors, Volume Computed Tomography Dose Index (CTDIvol) reflects scanner radiation output and is closely related to image noise, making it central to the image quality–dose relationship. Dose Length Product (DLP = CTDIvol × scan length) estimates the total radiation output across the entire scan. Total DLP, summed across all series, is proportional to the radiation energy imparted to the patient and is used to estimate effective dose ().

Efforts to reduce inter-observer variability in CT image assessment through structured scoring have been explored previously,. An analysis of various approaches was undertaken a few years ago in a pediatric CT study, which demonstrated that a well-designed subjective rating scale (0–4 or 1–5) coupled with explicit criteria can meaningfully reduce variability among radiologist readers \[, \], reinforcing subjective assessment as a powerful, clinically intuitive, and implementable approach. This concept aided the concept of Acceptable Quality Doses (AQD) \[\], which required an easy-to-practice approach to image quality assessment, recognizing that diagnostic image acceptance cannot be separated from radiation appropriateness. Subsequently, adopting the widely familiar consumer-facing 5-star paradigm, a clinically aligned 5-star CT image rating system incorporating radiation acceptance rather than sheer crispness was formalized \[\]. The system explicitly inverted the traditional assumption that lowest noise equals best quality, emphasizing that unjustifiably low image noise (usually implying high dose) should be rated down the scale, not up.

In 2024, it was decided to test this 5-star rating method through the IAEA Technical Cooperation (TC) Program in the Europe Region under project RER9157 in collaboration with the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH). This multinational study focused on different clinical indications for chest and abdominal CT examinations in adults (≥18 years). The present study builds on this effort by analyzing real-world CT image-quality scores. By evaluating variability and agreement trends, this work highlights the measurable operational impact of a standardized image quality-with-dose lens across a section of European countries, identifying optimized practices and future opportunities for implementing dose-optimized CT imaging and improving radiation protection of patients.

## 2\. Methods

Under the IAEA Project RER 9157 “Strengthening implementation of justified and optimized use of ionizing radiation in medicine”, this study was designed for participating member countries from the European region, and a Task was created on “Rating diagnostic image quality in consideration of dose” in cooperation with MGH, Harvard Medical School, with a radiologist expert from the UK assisting. Six countries decided to participate. They were asked to check if ethical committee/IRB clearance is required by their institution, and if so, get that approval for this retrospective study that does not require intervention with patient management, and thus obtain a waiver of informed consent. Only five countries could proceed.

CT examinations were collected across predefined chest and abdominal clinical indications. Three radiologists per institution independently assessed image quality using the standardized 5-star rating system after structured orientation. Data collected included scanner type, clinical indication, patient characteristics, CTDIvol, and total DLP. A representative image per examination was evaluated in relation to CTDIvol. Reference CTDIvol values of 8 mGy (chest) and 10 mGy (abdomen) were used to classify examinations with values below these thresholds as low-dose examinations. Inter-reader agreement and relationships between dose metrics, clinical indication, and image quality scores were analyzed. Image quality scoring was performed under routine diagnostic viewing conditions at each site, on clinical PACS workstations with diagnostic-grade monitors; readers at each institution were qualified radiologists experienced in chest and abdominal CT reporting with different levels of experience. Acquisition protocols were the routine clinical protocols in use at each participating site and were region and/or clinical indication specific (separate 11 clinical indications for chest region and 12 clinical indications for abdomen region). Apart from large-patient dedicated protocols reported by one center, they were generally not stratified by individual clinical indication, but rather by body regions, so that dose variation within a given indication largely reflects local protocol settings rather than indication-specific design. For the abdominal cohort, several indications (urinary bladder, prostate, colorectal cancer, and kidney stones) were acquired as abdomino-pelvic examinations. Because CTDIvol is a scan length independent output index, the 10 mGy reference applies irrespective of coverage, whereas the total DLP scales with scan length and number of series; CTDIvol and DLP are therefore reported separately, and the wider DLP spread seen for extended coverage indications should be interpreted accordingly. CTDIvol and DLP are standardized scanner output indices rather than measurements of absorbed or organ dose to the individual patient, and were used here as practical, universally available surrogates for relative dose comparison.

For this study, CTDIvol values of 8 mGy for chest and 10 mGy for abdominal examinations were selected as reference levels, based on the achievable dose values and DRLs values in published literature,. The correlation between the collected data (the type of scanners, clinical indications, CTDIvol range, and image quality score for each analyzed image) was illustrated as Sankey diagrams (generated using Python 3.12). The analysis was descriptive and observational by design. Inter-reader agreement was quantified as the proportion of examinations with full three-reader concordance and with pairwise (two of three) concordance, per hospital and per anatomical region. Given the ordinal five-category scale and the markedly unequal case numbers across sites and indications, summary statistics (median, mean, interquartile range) were preferred to modeled inferential testing, which would risk over-interpreting unbalanced subgroups. Reported values, therefore, carry the uncertainties inherent in retrospective, multicenter data of unequal size, including differences in case mix, scanner generation, reconstruction settings, and the incomplete documentation of some dosimetric and patient variables noted in the Results and Discussion.

## 3\. Results

The study included 1,247 chest and 1,490 abdominal CT examinations across 13 scanners, most installed between 2015 and 2024 (). While the utilization of intravenous contrast was prevalent (802 chest; 1191 abdomen), the documentation of essential dosimetric and patient metrics exhibited notable variability; Total Dose Length Product (DLP) and patient weight were recorded in 96.6% (1204/1247), and 70.2% (875/1247) of chest exams, respectively, but only in 60.3% (898/1490), and 78.3% (1166/1490) of abdominal cohorts. There were no dedicated protocols for obese patients in the majority (chest: 84% and abdomen: 93%) of the exams, indicating that CTDIvol was only adjusted predominantly by the Automatic Exposure Control (AEC). Only Hungary responded affirmative for separate protocols for obese patients > 100 kg in several chest and abdominal indications. Exams without recorded body weights were excluded from CTDIvol – weight category analysis.

Sub-analysis to evaluate the CTDIvol values and IQ scores by clinical indication was also performed. The IQ star rating had the highest variation for pneumothorax in the chest region, ranging from 1 to 5 stars, and for kidney stones in the abdomen region, ranging from 2 to 5 stars, indicating a protocol adjustment for low image noise in some cases.

Dosimetry analysis revealed pronounced heterogeneity in radiation indices within the same indication, with the greatest variance in CTDIvol observed for aortic dissection (mean CTDIvol: 21.9 mGy) in chest examinations. Furthermore, aortic dissection also exhibited a disproportionately elevated total DLP relative to their associated CTDIvol, probably due to large variation in the number of phases. Although recorded within the chest category, suspected aortic dissection is typically acquired with extended cranio-caudal coverage (frequently chest through abdomen and pelvis) and with multiple contrast phases; this extended coverage, rather than a higher per-rotation output alone, explains the disproportionately elevated DLP relative to CTDIvol for this indication.

Stratification of CTDIvol by specific clinical indications also revealed high variations across the chest cohort. As anticipated, higher CTDIvol values were noted for diagnostically demanding image quality like lung cancer, trauma (pneumothorax), and pulmonary embolism examinations, with mean CTDIvol values of 9.9 mGy, 9.9 mGy, and 9.0 mGy, respectively. Pulmonary nodule examinations, although intrinsically noise-tolerant, showed higher-than-expected CTDIvol (mean 7.5 mGy), exceeding values used for several other parenchymal indications, including sarcoidosis (7.2 mGy), pneumonia (6.7 mGy), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (6.1 mGy), and bronchiectasis (5.5 mGy).

Based on our findings on median CTDIvol values, we can recommend CTDIvol values as: 3.6 mGy, 5.9 mGy and 7.4 mGy for the corresponding weight groups (<60 kg, 60–79 kg and 80–99 kg), underscoring the scope for dose reduction in noise-tolerant chest indications. We are not able to recommend a CTDIvol value for chest examinations for patients weighing > 100 kg due to the limited number of cases > 100 kg in our study.

A similar trend was evident within the abdominal cohort as seen in the chest exams. The greatest variations in CTDIvol were found in urinary bladder cancer examinations (mean CTDIvol: 11.0 mGy). Unexpectedly, the mean CTDIvol value for kidney stones (10.0 mGy) was higher than for hepatocellular cancer (8 mGy), appendicitis (7.7 mGy), and cholangiocarcinoma (5.9 mGy) examinations. Based on our findings, we can recommend CTDIvol values as low as 4.2 mGy, 6.2 mGy and 8.4 mGy for the corresponding weight groups (< 60 kg, 60–79 kg, 80–99 kg), underscoring the scope for dose reduction in noise-tolerant abdominal indications. We are not able to recommend a CTDIvol value for abdominal examination for patients weighing > 100 kg due to the limited number of cases > 100 kg in our study.

The most profound variation with a disproportionately higher total DLP was observed in cholecystitis examinations. These observations indicate that protocol configurations and dose optimization in participating centers were dictated more by local institutional practices than by clinical indications or specific diagnostic requirements.

The analysis of the relationship between median CTDIvol values and subjective star ratings across patient weight groups () revealed a compelling inverse correlation in both chest and abdominal CT examinations. This trend was predominantly observed between 2- and 3-star scores and among patients with weights over 60 kg. Notably, in patients < 60 kg, the median CTDIvol for chest examinations plateaued across all star ratings, and showed a mild decreasing trend in abdominal exams. These findings may reflect the operational limit of the AEC systems, where low mA-pre-settings prevent the scanner from further reducing the dose in smaller habitus. The 2-star ratings (representing unnecessarily high-resolution, noise-free image quality) were consistently associated with higher radiation doses across nearly all weight groups, peaking near 16 mGy for abdominal scans in patients > 100 kg. As image quality trended towards 5-star (with appropriate tolerance of image noise), a marked reduction in CTDIvol was observed.

## 4\. Discussion

This multinational study validates the use of the structured 5-star IQ rating system which can be applied to multiple clinical indications, institutions and countries, providing concordant, dose-aware evaluation of CT image quality. Furthermore, use of the IQ rating system reveals important differences in exam protocols and institutional practices, and will help with exam optimization in line with national DRLs based on clinical indications.

A key observation from this work is that star-based rating is reproducible in institutions once readers are familiarized with the rating methodology. Hospitals such as CHC Rijeka in Croatia and the University Clinic of Surgery in North Macedonia demonstrated high levels of three-reader concordance for both chest and abdominal CT examinations. Even in institutions where full three-reader agreement was lower, pairwise agreement remained high and complete discordance was rare. These findings suggest that the 5-star method provides a simple and reliable way to translate subjective radiologist perception into structured, analyzable data suitable for large multicenter studies.

The study also illustrates an important principle of dose-aware imaging: acceptable diagnostic image quality can frequently be achieved at lower radiation dose when the clinical task is noise-tolerant**.** CT exams for indications such as pulmonary nodule assessment demonstrated relatively low CTDIvol values while maintaining high star ratings, reflecting the fact that clinical diagnosis relies on high intrinsic contrast, and remains robust even in the presence of visible image noise,. In abdominal examinations, pancreatic cancer and acute & chronic pancreatitis received the highest scores, 4 or 5. This observation reinforces the central concept of the rating system that the highest rating does not correspond to the highest resolution but to diagnostic quality images in the presence of acceptable noise for optimized radiation exposure.

Interestingly, chest CT images in larger patients had higher image ratings at a lower median CTDIvol than those in smaller patients. A plausible explanation is the natural contrast between mediastinal and perivascular fat in larger patients, which provides fat/soft-tissue contrast even in the presence of substantial image noise. Although this observation requires further investigation, it highlights how patient anatomy can independently influence perceived image quality, independent of radiation output. However, the above explanation is not applicable to all involved CT departments, as only the University of Szeged reported having dedicated protocols for large patients for some chest and abdomen examinations.

Based on DLP values, the analysis also identified substantial variability in protocols for abdominal clinical indications, particularly CT for kidney stones and suspected appendicitis. In some institutions, such CT was performed with higher CTDIvol values despite the well-established feasibility and recommendations for using very low-dose protocols for this indication,. Given the high prevalence of these indications, protocol harmonization could achieve meaningful dose reduction without compromising diagnostic performance. Optimizing protocols for these indications could yield substantial dose reduction without compromising diagnostic information, and may be an area where coordinated guidance from national authorities and international organizations could be beneficial. In practical terms, the rating output can be used at three levels: at the reader level, to flag examinations rated two stars (unnecessarily low noise) for protocol review; at the department level, to compare the star distribution and paired CTDIvol across indications and to identify noise-tolerant examinations (kidney stones, pulmonary nodules, appendicitis) still performed above achievable doses; and at the network or national level, to target harmonization of the specific indication and scanner combinations that concentrate at the low-star, high-dose end.

Disproportionately higher DLP values with respect to CTDIvol for indications such as suspected aortic dissection, cholecystitis, appendicitis, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer are shown by wider interquartile ranges or high outliers. The relatively low CTDIvol but higher total DLP values in those exams may be explained by multiple acquisitions (phases) or longer scan ranges. Whereas a multiphase protocol is appropriate for many of these indications, such as aortic dissection, the use of multiple acquisitions/phases should only be in line with best practice. Optimization of imaging protocols in line with guidance would, again bring benefits.

Finally, the study highlights the potential role of CT manufacturers in setting dose-optimized protocols. Differences in reconstruction algorithms, kernel selection, and vendor-specific image processing can substantially influence the balance between noise, and perceived image quality, and therefore have an impact on the patient dose. Continued collaboration between manufacturers, clinical users, and international organizations may help refine reconstruction strategies that allow lower radiation output while maintaining diagnostic confidence. Improvements in iterative reconstruction, and emerging AI-based reconstruction techniques may further support this goal.

The limitations of the study include a lack of information on the availability of noise-reduction post-processing software and the exclusion of a small number of cases with non-concordance, where all three radiologists gave different ratings. Also, a limitation of the 5-star rating system is that it does not explicitly differentiate examinations with higher radiation dose but suboptimal image quality due to technical or equipment-related factors. Future refinements of the scoring framework may benefit from explicitly distinguishing technical or equipment-related causes of suboptimal image quality from dose-related optimization. It should be emphasized that high inter-reader agreement demonstrates the reproducibility of the rating instrument, not the appropriateness of the underlying protocols: a center can reach near-unanimous agreement while consistently operating at unnecessarily high dose, as illustrated by the predominance of concordant two-star ratings at one site. The observed dose variation reflects a combination of intrinsic sources (the diagnostic task and patient habitus) and modifiable sources (local protocol choices). The weight-stratified analysis () partially separates these by holding patient size approximately constant, but residual confounding from case mix, scanner generation, and reconstruction cannot be fully excluded, and a formal protocol-level optimization analysis was beyond the scope of this observational study.

These findings demonstrate that integrating structured image quality assessment with dose metrics through a 5-star rating enables the identification of unnecessarily high radiation protocols and supports the implementation of dose-optimized CT imaging across diverse CT facilities.

[^1]: ## 1\. Introduction

The exponential global volume of CT imaging has been paralleled by an increasing clinical demand for measurable image quality and responsible radiation use. However, unlike laboratory image analysis, there is no internationally harmonized method for radiologists to score diagnostic CT image quality in routine practice. The absence of standardization in image quality assessment carries measurable consequences, including unwarranted radiation exposure from repeat imaging, loss of diagnostic confidence, sub-optimal protocol design, and missed opportunities for systematic dose reduction. Objective physical metrics of CT image quality, such as noise, contrast-to-noise ratio, and spatial resolution, are well established, but they do not directly reflect clinical diagnostic acceptability. Consequently, subjective assessment methods, often based on Likert-type scales, remain essential in routine practice. There have been many attempts to achieve subjective assessment of image quality, mostly based on the Likert scale.

Among the available dose descriptors, Volume Computed Tomography Dose Index (CTDIvol) reflects scanner radiation output and is closely related to image noise, making it central to the image quality–dose relationship. Dose Length Product (DLP = CTDIvol × scan length) estimates the total radiation output across the entire scan. Total DLP, summed across all series, is proportional to the radiation energy imparted to the patient and is used to estimate effective dose ().

Efforts to reduce inter-observer variability in CT image assessment through structured scoring have been explored previously,. An analysis of various approaches was undertaken a few years ago in a pediatric CT study, which demonstrated that a well-designed subjective rating scale (0–4 or 1–5) coupled with explicit criteria can meaningfully reduce variability among radiologist readers \[, \], reinforcing subjective assessment as a powerful, clinically intuitive, and implementable approach. This concept aided the concept of Acceptable Quality Doses (AQD) \[\], which required an easy-to-practice approach to image quality assessment, recognizing that diagnostic image acceptance cannot be separated from radiation appropriateness. Subsequently, adopting the widely familiar consumer-facing 5-star paradigm, a clinically aligned 5-star CT image rating system incorporating radiation acceptance rather than sheer crispness was formalized \[\]. The system explicitly inverted the traditional assumption that lowest noise equals best quality, emphasizing that unjustifiably low image noise (usually implying high dose) should be rated down the scale, not up.

In 2024, it was decided to test this 5-star rating method through the IAEA Technical Cooperation (TC) Program in the Europe Region under project RER9157 in collaboration with the Massachusetts General Hospital (MGH). This multinational study focused on different clinical indications for chest and abdominal CT examinations in adults (≥18 years). The present study builds on this effort by analyzing real-world CT image-quality scores. By evaluating variability and agreement trends, this work highlights the measurable operational impact of a standardized image quality-with-dose lens across a section of European countries, identifying optimized practices and future opportunities for implementing dose-optimized CT imaging and improving radiation protection of patients.

## 2\. Methods

Under the IAEA Project RER 9157 “Strengthening implementation of justified and optimized use of ionizing radiation in medicine”, this study was designed for participating member countries from the European region, and a Task was created on “Rating diagnostic image quality in consideration of dose” in cooperation with MGH, Harvard Medical School, with a radiologist expert from the UK assisting. Six countries decided to participate. They were asked to check if ethical committee/IRB clearance is required by their institution, and if so, get that approval for this retrospective study that does not require intervention with patient management, and thus obtain a waiver of informed consent. Only five countries could proceed.

CT examinations were collected across predefined chest and abdominal clinical indications. Three radiologists per institution independently assessed image quality using the standardized 5-star rating system after structured orientation. Data collected included scanner type, clinical indication, patient characteristics, CTDIvol, and total DLP. A representative image per examination was evaluated in relation to CTDIvol. Reference CTDIvol values of 8 mGy (chest) and 10 mGy (abdomen) were used to classify examinations with values below these thresholds as low-dose examinations. Inter-reader agreement and relationships between dose metrics, clinical indication, and image quality scores were analyzed. Image quality scoring was performed under routine diagnostic viewing conditions at each site, on clinical PACS workstations with diagnostic-grade monitors; readers at each institution were qualified radiologists experienced in chest and abdominal CT reporting with different levels of experience. Acquisition protocols were the routine clinical protocols in use at each participating site and were region and/or clinical indication specific (separate 11 clinical indications for chest region and 12 clinical indications for abdomen region). Apart from large-patient dedicated protocols reported by one center, they were generally not stratified by individual clinical indication, but rather by body regions, so that dose variation within a given indication largely reflects local protocol settings rather than indication-specific design. For the abdominal cohort, several indications (urinary bladder, prostate, colorectal cancer, and kidney stones) were acquired as abdomino-pelvic examinations. Because CTDIvol is a scan length independent output index, the 10 mGy reference applies irrespective of coverage, whereas the total DLP scales with scan length and number of series; CTDIvol and DLP are therefore reported separately, and the wider DLP spread seen for extended coverage indications should be interpreted accordingly. CTDIvol and DLP are standardized scanner output indices rather than measurements of absorbed or organ dose to the individual patient, and were used here as practical, universally available surrogates for relative dose comparison.

For this study, CTDIvol values of 8 mGy for chest and 10 mGy for abdominal examinations were selected as reference levels, based on the achievable dose values and DRLs values in published literature,. The correlation between the collected data (the type of scanners, clinical indications, CTDIvol range, and image quality score for each analyzed image) was illustrated as Sankey diagrams (generated using Python 3.12). The analysis was descriptive and observational by design. Inter-reader agreement was quantified as the proportion of examinations with full three-reader concordance and with pairwise (two of three) concordance, per hospital and per anatomical region. Given the ordinal five-category scale and the markedly unequal case numbers across sites and indications, summary statistics (median, mean, interquartile range) were preferred to modeled inferential testing, which would risk over-interpreting unbalanced subgroups. Reported values, therefore, carry the uncertainties inherent in retrospective, multicenter data of unequal size, including differences in case mix, scanner generation, reconstruction settings, and the incomplete documentation of some dosimetric and patient variables noted in the Results and Discussion.

## 4\. Discussion

This multinational study validates the use of the structured 5-star IQ rating system which can be applied to multiple clinical indications, institutions and countries, providing concordant, dose-aware evaluation of CT image quality. Furthermore, use of the IQ rating system reveals important differences in exam protocols and institutional practices, and will help with exam optimization in line with national DRLs based on clinical indications.

A key observation from this work is that star-based rating is reproducible in institutions once readers are familiarized with the rating methodology. Hospitals such as CHC Rijeka in Croatia and the University Clinic of Surgery in North Macedonia demonstrated high levels of three-reader concordance for both chest and abdominal CT examinations. Even in institutions where full three-reader agreement was lower, pairwise agreement remained high and complete discordance was rare. These findings suggest that the 5-star method provides a simple and reliable way to translate subjective radiologist perception into structured, analyzable data suitable for large multicenter studies.

The study also illustrates an important principle of dose-aware imaging: acceptable diagnostic image quality can frequently be achieved at lower radiation dose when the clinical task is noise-tolerant**.** CT exams for indications such as pulmonary nodule assessment demonstrated relatively low CTDIvol values while maintaining high star ratings, reflecting the fact that clinical diagnosis relies on high intrinsic contrast, and remains robust even in the presence of visible image noise,. In abdominal examinations, pancreatic cancer and acute & chronic pancreatitis received the highest scores, 4 or 5. This observation reinforces the central concept of the rating system that the highest rating does not correspond to the highest resolution but to diagnostic quality images in the presence of acceptable noise for optimized radiation exposure.

Interestingly, chest CT images in larger patients had higher image ratings at a lower median CTDIvol than those in smaller patients. A plausible explanation is the natural contrast between mediastinal and perivascular fat in larger patients, which provides fat/soft-tissue contrast even in the presence of substantial image noise. Although this observation requires further investigation, it highlights how patient anatomy can independently influence perceived image quality, independent of radiation output. However, the above explanation is not applicable to all involved CT departments, as only the University of Szeged reported having dedicated protocols for large patients for some chest and abdomen examinations.

Based on DLP values, the analysis also identified substantial variability in protocols for abdominal clinical indications, particularly CT for kidney stones and suspected appendicitis. In some institutions, such CT was performed with higher CTDIvol values despite the well-established feasibility and recommendations for using very low-dose protocols for this indication,. Given the high prevalence of these indications, protocol harmonization could achieve meaningful dose reduction without compromising diagnostic performance. Optimizing protocols for these indications could yield substantial dose reduction without compromising diagnostic information, and may be an area where coordinated guidance from national authorities and international organizations could be beneficial. In practical terms, the rating output can be used at three levels: at the reader level, to flag examinations rated two stars (unnecessarily low noise) for protocol review; at the department level, to compare the star distribution and paired CTDIvol across indications and to identify noise-tolerant examinations (kidney stones, pulmonary nodules, appendicitis) still performed above achievable doses; and at the network or national level, to target harmonization of the specific indication and scanner combinations that concentrate at the low-star, high-dose end.

Disproportionately higher DLP values with respect to CTDIvol for indications such as suspected aortic dissection, cholecystitis, appendicitis, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer are shown by wider interquartile ranges or high outliers. The relatively low CTDIvol but higher total DLP values in those exams may be explained by multiple acquisitions (phases) or longer scan ranges. Whereas a multiphase protocol is appropriate for many of these indications, such as aortic dissection, the use of multiple acquisitions/phases should only be in line with best practice. Optimization of imaging protocols in line with guidance would, again bring benefits.

Finally, the study highlights the potential role of CT manufacturers in setting dose-optimized protocols. Differences in reconstruction algorithms, kernel selection, and vendor-specific image processing can substantially influence the balance between noise, and perceived image quality, and therefore have an impact on the patient dose. Continued collaboration between manufacturers, clinical users, and international organizations may help refine reconstruction strategies that allow lower radiation output while maintaining diagnostic confidence. Improvements in iterative reconstruction, and emerging AI-based reconstruction techniques may further support this goal.

The limitations of the study include a lack of information on the availability of noise-reduction post-processing software and the exclusion of a small number of cases with non-concordance, where all three radiologists gave different ratings. Also, a limitation of the 5-star rating system is that it does not explicitly differentiate examinations with higher radiation dose but suboptimal image quality due to technical or equipment-related factors. Future refinements of the scoring framework may benefit from explicitly distinguishing technical or equipment-related causes of suboptimal image quality from dose-related optimization. It should be emphasized that high inter-reader agreement demonstrates the reproducibility of the rating instrument, not the appropriateness of the underlying protocols: a center can reach near-unanimous agreement while consistently operating at unnecessarily high dose, as illustrated by the predominance of concordant two-star ratings at one site. The observed dose variation reflects a combination of intrinsic sources (the diagnostic task and patient habitus) and modifiable sources (local protocol choices). The weight-stratified analysis () partially separates these by holding patient size approximately constant, but residual confounding from case mix, scanner generation, and reconstruction cannot be fully excluded, and a formal protocol-level optimization analysis was beyond the scope of this observational study.

These findings demonstrate that integrating structured image quality assessment with dose metrics through a 5-star rating enables the identification of unnecessarily high radiation protocols and supports the implementation of dose-optimized CT imaging across diverse CT facilities.
