## Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) is revolutionizing many scientific fields. AI techniques are more successful today largely due to the tremendous growth in computational power and the availability of vast\, diverse datasets, which allow advanced models to learn complex patterns and make highly accurate predictions across various applications. In healthcare, AI is enabling breakthroughs in many areas where, for instance, AI-driven image analysis assists clinicians in identifying conditions with remarkable speed and accuracy. These innovations are revolutionizing patient care by equipping clinicians with the advanced tools to make data-driven decisions, thereby enhancing patient outcomes and boosting overall healthcare efficiency.

AI has become a cornerstone in biomedical engineering, particularly in the field of biomedical imaging, where it aids in analyzing complex medical data \[\]. Machine learning (ML), and in particular\, deep learning (DL), a more advanced and layered approach, excels at automatically detecting intricate patterns in large, sophisticated datasets. Recent research in biomedical imaging has increasingly focused on DL due to its ability to handle complex image data and achieve high accuracy in detecting, classifying, and segmenting medical conditions \[\], where deep neural network architectures have demonstrated remarkable success.

Biomedical imaging is considered the most essential tool for diagnosing and analyzing a wide range of diseases \[\]. illustrates two primary modes by which AI solutions can be integrated into disease detection workflows: the sequential mode (light brown solid line) and the concurrent mode (green dashed line) \[\]. In the sequential mode, AI analysis is additionally performed, with results subsequently reviewed and verified by the clinician. In other words, the clinician and AI both receive and process the same input data independently, with the AI's output then reviewed by the clinician in a sequential second-reader workflow. This approach can enhance diagnostic safety by providing an additional layer of verification, but may increase overall processing time. In contrast, the concurrent mode allows AI to provide supplementary information to the clinician in real time during the diagnostic process, potentially accelerating decision-making and improving efficiency. However, concurrent integration may introduce challenges such as alarm fatigue, clinician over-reliance on AI outputs, and workflow disruptions if the system is not seamlessly embedded into clinical practice. These workflow variations directly relate to the broader issues discussed in detail in ‘Key challenges and issues’ section, where transparency, interpretability, and proper training are emphasized as prerequisites for successful AI adoption in healthcare environments.

Over the past few years, DL models, especially convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and more recent architectures such as transformers, have brought transformative changes to biomedical imaging, improving screening and detection of various medical conditions from diverse imaging modalities such as chest X-ray (CXR), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), computed tomography (CT) scan, and ultrasound, among others \[\]. In addition, AI's capacity to integrate multi-modal medical data \[\] and generate explainable insights \[\] has not only increased diagnostic precision but also fostered trust in machine-aided decision-making among healthcare professionals. The impact of these advances has been far-reaching, making healthcare more proactive and personalized, ultimately enhancing patient outcomes and supporting a data-driven future in medicine.

This short review synthesizes recent advancements in AI-powered biomedical imaging based on a targeted literature search conducted between January 2020 and June 2025 across PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar. We prioritized peer-reviewed journal articles, conference proceedings, and preprints that made significant contributions to the field, with selection criteria including relevance to emerging techniques, representative examples of impactful clinical or technical applications, and explicit discussion of key challenges. The intent was to provide an accessible, high-level overview of notable developments and trends rather than an exhaustive or systematic literature synthesis. Accordingly, this paper reviews the impact of AI advancements in biomedical imaging in recent years, offering an insightful snapshot of techniques and applications while also exploring the challenges associated with AI implementation and integration. That is, the paper adopts a narrative review approach guided by relevance and representativeness, focusing on key recent contributions that exemplify significant methodological advances and clinical impact in AI-powered biomedical imaging. By spotlighting specific innovations and highlighting recent research trends, our objective is to equip researchers and clinicians with a concise yet informed understanding of AI's transformative role in improving healthcare outcomes.

Several recent reviews have surveyed AI applications in biomedical imaging from various perspectives, including broad overviews of DL methods for medical image analysis \[,\], multimodal data integration \[\], explainable AI techniques \[\], and the role of AI in specific clinical domains \[\]. While our work naturally overlaps with these efforts in covering core DL techniques and their biomedical applications, the present review differs in both *scope* and *intent*. This is a concise, high-level synthesis focusing specifically on the most recent advances from 2020 to 2025, selected for their representativeness, clinical relevance, and methodological novelty. In contrast to exhaustive or domain-specific surveys (e.g. Refs. \[,,\]), our review integrates multiple emerging themes—including advances in segmentation\, diagnostic modeling, synthetic data generation, explainable AI, and multimodal foundation models—into a single coherent narrative that connects these developments to their shared goal of improving clinical imaging practice and patient outcomes. We also emphasize practical deployment considerations (e.g., model lifecycle and clinician training), which are often underexplored in prior works. We hope this review serves as an up-to-date and application-oriented resource for both technical researchers and clinical practitioners seeking to understand state-of-the-art developments and their real-world implications.

Subsequently, the ‘Key challenges and issues’ section reviews the main issues and considerations involved in implementing AI-driven solutions in healthcare. The paper concludes in the ‘Conclusion’ section.

## Artificial intelligence applications in biomedical imaging

AI has experienced a swift transformation in recent years, particularly in biomedical imaging\, driven by advances in ML and DL techniques. The growing adoption of these technologies stems from their ability to process vast and complex medical datasets—including multi-modal imaging data from different modalities such as MRI, CT, CXR, and ultrasound—and to extract clinically relevant insights that were previously unattainable \[\]. In this context, ML focuses on developing algorithms that enable systems to autonomously learn diagnostic patterns from medical data without explicit programming, while DL, a specialized subset of ML, employs multilayer neural networks designed to capture intricate anatomical and pathological features within imaging studies. Through iterative training\, deep neural networks (DNNs) progressively refine their accuracy, enabling various applications such as tumor segmentation\, disease classification, and image reconstruction. Their architecture—characterized by multiple layers bridging input and output—facilitates complex data representation learning, making DNNs particularly adept at tackling medical imaging challenges such as noise artifacts and high-dimensional spatial information. These advances directly address critical medical needs including improving early disease detection, accelerating image interpretation to support timely interventions in acute care, and enhancing diagnostic accuracy in resource-limited settings where specialist expertise may be scarce. AI-powered solutions also facilitate longitudinal monitoring of disease progression, personalized treatment planning, and integration of imaging with other clinical data to support precision medicine approaches. These capabilities have led to significant improvements in screening\, diagnostics, and treatment planning, illustrating AI's transformative role in modern biomedical imaging.

The studies highlighted in this section were deliberately selected as representative examples across diverse modalities, learning paradigms, and validation approaches to illustrate recent diagnostic trends, rather than to provide an exhaustive catalog of all available work.

### Image segmentation

Recent progress in DL has notably advanced the field of image segmentation, a process that entails the precise delineation of anatomical structures to attain optimal accuracy. Accurate segmentation is vital for clinical applications, e.g.\, disease monitoring and diagnosis. Approaches utilizing CNNs are predominant. The initial and most straightforward methods for segmentation with CNNs involved classifying individual pixels within small image patches. In 2015, the introduction of the U-Net architecture \[\] impacted medical image segmentation, paving the way for the creation of various enhanced versions of the encoder-decoder architecture to further boost its effectiveness. For instance, the nnU-Net (no new U-Net) framework, which builds upon the encoder-decoder structure of U-Net, autonomously configures pre-processing, network architecture, training, inference, and post-processing strategies tailored to any dataset \[\]. While these techniques have set new benchmarks in various medical segmentation tasks, they require significant amounts of high-quality annotated data for effective training.

To tackle the challenges of labeling in medical image segmentation, significant efforts have been directed toward creating annotation-efficient DL methods. These approaches aim to expand training data by generating labels, augmenting data, utilizing related external labeled datasets, and employing semi-supervised learning to leverage unlabeled data. Among these strategies, semi-supervised segmentation (e.g., Refs. \[,\]) is notably practical as it enables models to effectively utilize readily available unlabeled data in conjunction with a limited set of labeled data for training \[\]. However, a major limitation of many medical image segmentation models is their task-specific design, which often results in a notable decline in performance when applied to new tasks or different types of imaging data \[\]. Recently, foundation models, i.e., models that can be trained once and subsequently utilized for a broad spectrum of segmentation tasks, have been introduced to address this limitation by facilitating universal image segmentation. Although general-purpose segmentation foundation models, such as Segment Anything Model (SAM) \[\] and SAM 2 \[\], have been employed in various medical studies, their application in medical image segmentation is constrained by the substantial differences between natural and medical images. To overcome this limitation, very recently, specialized foundation models for medical images, e.g., MedSAM \[\] and Medical SAM 2 \[\], have been developed by fine-tuning general-purpose models with extensive medical datasets. summarizes the representative AI methods discussed in this section, their main biomedical imaging applications, and contributions.

### Healthcare diagnostics

AI is transforming healthcare diagnostics by providing advanced solutions in medical imaging, which have significantly improved both accuracy and efficiency \[\]. Conventional diagnostic methods that rely on radiologists can be both time-intensive and prone to human error, and they may not be readily available, especially in areas with limited resources \[\]. Computer vision and AI in medical imaging for diagnostics have seen a significant increase in scientific publications, particularly in radiology, pathology, ophthalmology, and dermatology \[\].

Various imaging modalities, such as X-ray radiography, CT, ultrasound, and MRI, are routinely used for diagnostics in healthcare \[\]. Recently, DNNs have demonstrated significant success in automating the analysis of different medical imaging modalities. These efforts have initially centered around CNNs which can analyze the spatial and temporal features of images to extract hidden patterns and meaningful information \[\], targeting different diseases, e.g., COVID-19 screening \[\] and early detection of Alzheimer's disease \[\], to name a few. They have also been applied for other purposes in clinical settings including enhancing patient safety, monitoring patient deterioration, quantifying pain, and aiding in the mobilization of critically ill patients \[\], among others.

Researchers have continually proposed architectural enhancements to improve CNNs, with one significant advancement being the introduction of the attention mechanism \[\]. Initially employed in natural language processing (NLP), this mechanism allows the network to prioritize specific image regions and process them through deeper layers more effectively. In 2020, vision transformer (ViT) \[\] was introduced as an adaptation of the transformer model designed to process sequences of image patches for detailed feature extraction. ViTs have been successfully applied to a range of medical image classification problems across different modalities including detecting cancer \[\], classifying pneumonia \[\], and tuberculosis screening \[\].

Few-shot and zero-shot learning techniques have recently emerged in response to the challenges of acquiring the extensive medical imaging datasets typically required for conventional DL approaches. Few-shot learning (FSL) enables models to learn from a limited number of observations, similar to human learning, allowing effective learning with minimal labeled data \[\]. Different techniques, architectures, and strategies have been applied to various clinical problems including disease screening and classification. Examples are, prototypical network for COVID-19 screening \[\], breast cancer classification using a siamese network \[\], and self-supervised tuberculosis screening \[\], among others. Foundation models utilizing self-supervised and zero-shot learning also offer a promising alternative to classification tasks by reducing the reliance on large, task-specific labeled datasets required by traditional DNNs \[\]. An example is \[\], which introduced CheXzero, showcasing that self-supervised learning (SSL) can achieve radiologist-level accuracy in classifying pathologies from CXR images without needing explicit annotations. provides an overview of the key methods presented in this section, highlighting their potential applications in biomedical imaging and their features.

### Synthetic data generation

AI models designed for medical imaging tasks often demand massive datasets. However, accessing these datasets presents challenges, e.g., privacy and ethical concerns, making them scarce and difficult to compile. Therefore, synthetic medical imaging data generation has emerged as a promising solution (). Generative adversarial networks (GANs) are pivotal in synthetic medical imaging generation \[\], with several variations proposed. For instance, enhanced balancing GAN \[\] tackles the problem of data imbalance by generating images for the minority class. Another example is an attention-based GAN, called BliMSR \[\], a technique to generate enhanced medical images by modeling degradation in a data-driven manner. Another category of GAN variants including progressively growing GANs \[\] and style distribution GAN (SD-GAN) \[\], specializes in producing clinically realistic X-rays.

### Explainability

To boost trust and acceptance of AI solutions in clinical settings, significant efforts are underway to address the black-box problem with eXplainable AI (XAI) methods, which aim to clarify the ‘hows and whys’ behind deep neural networks. Recently, several XAI techniques have been introduced to improve the interpretability of DNNs in medical imaging. These techniques generally fall into four categories: 1) Perturbation-based, 2) Gradient-based, 3) Decomposition, and 4) Content-based methods. While critics argue that models should be designed to be inherently interpretable from the outset, rather than relying on post-hoc XAI techniques to interpret black-box models \[\], these post-hoc techniques have nonetheless proven valuable in specific healthcare domains.

*Perturbation-based* methods assess feature importance by examining how changes in input affect model outputs. By modifying specific regions of an image, these methods pinpoint areas that have a significant impact on predictions. These methods are crucial for examining CNNs and understanding their behavior in safety-critical areas where transparency is essential \[\]. Various techniques within this category such as integrated gradients (IG) \[\] and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) \[\], have been utilized in diverse medical applications, e.g., identifying congestive heart failure in chest radiographs \[\].

Backpropagation is a method for adjusting neural network weights during training. In *gradient-based* techniques, backpropagation is also used to compute gradients, where these gradients highlight important areas within an image without altering the weights \[\]. Different gradient-based techniques have been used for medical image analysis. Saliency maps \[\] utilize gradient information to elucidate the process by which deep convolutional networks classify images. The guided backpropagation method \[\] discards gradients when either the forward activation or the backward gradient is negative, thereby producing heatmaps that highlight pixels positively influencing classification. Class activation mapping (CAM) \[\] is another technique that emphasizes regions of an image that have a substantial influence on a neural network's classification decision. Additionally, Grad-CAM \[\] extends CAM's usability to various network architectures and outputs. presents an example of a Grad-CAM annotation, highlighting clinically relevant artifacts within a COVID-19-positive ultrasound image \[\].

*Decomposition-based* techniques break down a model's prediction by creating a heatmap that depicts the contribution of each pixel to the final decision. Layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) \[\] is one of these techniques that calculates relevance scores for each neuron by distributing the output of the final layer back through the network, rather than using gradients, to create a relevancy heatmap. Other common techniques in this category include softmax-gradient layer-wise relevance propagation (SGLRP) \[\] and concept relevance propagation (CRP) \[\], to name a few.

The XAI approaches mentioned above emphasize attributing the model's output to specific regions within the input image. In contrast, *concept-based* methods offer a global explanation by linking human-understandable concepts to high-level features learned by the network \[\]. Examples of such techniques include (but are not limited to) concept activation vectors (CAVs) \[\], automatic concept-based explanation (ACE) \[\], causal concept effect (CaCE) \[\], and ConceptSHAP \[\]. summarizes the techniques discussed in this section, outlining their applications in biomedical imaging and distinctive features.

While post-hoc XAI methods, such as Grad-CAM, have advanced our ability to visualize model reasoning, an important limitation is that these technical outputs do not always translate into clinically interpretable insights. Such visualizations often lack formal validation against clinicians' diagnostic reasoning, which can limit their utility in practice. Despite its widespread use, Grad-CAM is known to produce unstable saliency maps that may vary even under consistent model predictions and can be susceptible to adversarial perturbations, raising concerns about the reliability and robustness of visual explanations in safety-critical clinical settings. Moreover, a good medical AI explanation should be tailored to the user's expertise—while a visual disease localization may suffice for radiologists or imaging researchers, clinicians may need XAI integrated into a broader clinical decision-making framework that includes patient history, treatments, options, and expected outcomes \[\]. Clinician-in-the-loop approaches offer a promising pathway to bridge this gap. Embedding domain concepts into explanations not only improves interpretability but also facilitates trust, regulatory acceptance, and safe integration into healthcare workflows.

Moreover, beyond classic saliency-based visualizations, recent advances in XAI for medical imaging have explored methods that assess faithfulness and robustness of explanations, as well as generate counterfactual and simulatability-based interpretations. Faithfulness tests ensure that explanations accurately reflect the model's decision-making process, while robustness analyses examine stability under perturbations. Counterfactual approaches such as calibration-based counterfactuals for clinical predictions (TraCE) \[\], smooth counterfactual generation in CXR diagnosis \[\], and counterfactual image explanation using adversarial image-to-image translation techniques (GANterfactual) \[\], allow clinicians to explore how small, targeted changes in input data could alter diagnostic outcomes, thereby improving trust and actionable insight. Prototype-based XAI methods including XProtoNet \[\] and pseudo-class part-prototype networks (PCPPN) for fine-grained disease classification \[\], provide interpretable reasoning by referencing representative image regions from the training set that are most similar to the query case, offering local and global explanations. Another emerging approach is the concept bottleneck model (CBM) paradigm \[,\], which constrains the model to first predict a set of human-understandable intermediate concepts before making a final decision, thereby enabling direct clinical validation of the decision pathway. Together, these methods aim to complement traditional saliency maps by offering richer, more reliable, and clinically meaningful explanations that align with medical reasoning processes.

### Emerging artificial intelligence trends in biomedical imaging

Foundation models (FMs) represent a significant shift from traditional task-specific models as they are adaptable for various tasks beyond their initial training, having transformative potential in healthcare. FMs possess robust generative abilities and can effectively integrate diverse data modalities. This integration allows for the exploration of biomedical concepts across various scales and facilitates knowledge transfer between different data modalities \[\]. However, FMs introduce distinct challenges including the potential for over-generalization and ethical issues, necessitating meticulous implementation and ongoing oversight \[\]. Given the potential and benefits of FMs, research is actively exploring their integration with other approaches. For instance, a recent potential direction is the integration of FMs with federated learning, which provides a decentralized method for training models across multiple nodes or devices, emphasizing privacy \[\].

Vision-based medical foundation models, e.g., SAM-Med3D \[\], have tailored image-based architectures to accommodate a range of medical imaging modalities. A recent advancement to go beyond the task-specific paradigm is the development of multimodal foundation models, which integrate various data types, such as images, text, video, and audio, blending diverse architectures to achieve a comprehensive multimodal understanding \[\]. Some notable examples of these models include SurgVLP \[\] and OneLLM \[\]. This recent progress focuses on creating generalist medical AI (GMAI), an advanced type of medical foundation model designed to perform a variety of tasks with minimal or no task-specific labeled data \[\]. GMAI models have three distinct capabilities: 1) they can easily adapt to new tasks by understanding descriptions in plain language without retraining; 2) they can flexibly interact with varying combinations of data modalities for inputs and outputs, unlike rigid multimodal models; and 3) they encapsulate medical knowledge, allowing them to navigate unfamiliar tasks and employ accurate medical terminology to articulate their outputs. This adaptability enables GMAI models to remain effective in evolving environments and keep up with new diseases and technologies, avoiding the need for complete retraining \[\]. One may note that although GMAI models hold transformative potential for medical AI applications, their development and deployment are associated with significant computational complexities. Training such models often requires large clusters and distributed pipelines to handle multimodal data at scale \[\]. Moreover, inference latency remains a critical challenge for real-time clinical adoption, necessitating optimizations such as quantization, pruning, and hardware-specific acceleration \[\]. Additionally, the energy footprint of large-scale training raises sustainability concerns, highlighting the need for efficient architectures and resource-aware training strategies.

In addition to GMAI, vision-language foundation models represent a rapidly growing area in medical imaging AI. Vision-language models, leverage self- and semi-supervised learning to integrate medical imaging and textual data for tasks such as classification, segmentation, and report generation, reducing annotation needs, improving generalization, and streamlining diagnostic workflows \[\]. It is argued that foundation models in radiology can reduce data annotation bottlenecks and improve fairness and generalization by leveraging large unlabeled datasets, but they still face transparency and bias challenges, requiring decentralized clinical validation and continuous training frameworks to ensure responsible, adaptive deployment \[\].

Finally, we want to emphasize AI's potential to enhance real-time and point-of-care imaging, thus increasing accessibility. A notable example is AI in point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), given the growing compactness and portability of ultrasound devices \[\]. Clinicians are increasingly using POCUS for specific clinical inquiries both at the bedside and in remote areas \[\], but inadequate training remains a major obstacle preventing physicians from maximizing its use \[\]. AI-powered solutions can minimize operator dependency and shorten the learning curve for acquiring POCUS skills \[\], facilitating its adoption by more clinicians. This field is still in its early stages \[\], and the effectiveness of certain applications is yet to be fully understood.

## Key challenges and issues

AI has the potential to revolutionize clinical practice and improve patient outcomes. However, several challenges need to be addressed. This section explores the main obstacles.

### Data challenges

Diverse, high-quality medical imaging datasets are required for these deep data-hungry models, which are less available in the medical field than in the general domain. Comprehensive public datasets (similar to the MIMIC \[\]) are very limited yet crucial for effectively building AI models in healthcare applications. Building and managing these datasets necessitates partnerships among multiple parties, such as researchers, healthcare practitioners, and institutions \[\].

### Privacy and ethical considerations

Accessing medical data involves navigating both technical challenges and legal and ethical complexities \[\]. The need for high-quality large-scale data for training DNNs against protecting sensitive patient data presents a multi-faceted challenge \[\]. It is essential to implement well-established data governance policies, utilize state-of-the-art anonymization techniques, and adhere to regulatory frameworks to safeguard patient privacy \[\]. In addition, in real-world clinical settings\, data is often imbalanced, featuring an uneven distribution of conditions\, diseases\, demographics, etc \[\]. It is crucial to ensure that models are developed to be both robust and free from bias. A fundamental approach to safeguarding sensitive medical information is differential privacy (DP), which introduces controlled randomness into data or model parameters to ensure that individual patient records cannot be inferred from aggregate outputs \[\]. While DP focuses on preserving confidentiality in centralized data processing, emerging decentralized approaches such as federated learning (FL) extend this principle by enabling collaborative training across institutions without sharing raw data. As an increasingly important privacy-preserving approach in biomedical imaging, FL enables collaborative model training across multiple institutions without exchanging raw patient data. In FL, models are trained locally on site-specific datasets, and only the learned parameters are shared and aggregated, thereby safeguarding sensitive information and complying with regulatory frameworks \[\]. This decentralized paradigm is particularly valuable in multi-institutional settings, where heterogeneity in imaging protocols, patient demographics, and disease prevalence can otherwise hinder model generalizability. Studies have shown that FL can enhance cross-institutional validation by reducing performance degradation when AI systems are deployed in unseen clinical environments \[\]. Nonetheless, challenges remain including communication overhead\, domain shift across participating institutions, and the need for harmonized data annotation standards. Addressing these issues is essential for enabling privacy-conscious, generalizable AI solutions capable of safe and effective real-world deployment. In summary, several legal and ethical issues need to be considered such as those related to medical liability, maintaining transparency and traceability, providing explainability, managing sensitive health data, adhering to regulatory compliance, addressing product liability, and securing malpractice insurance \[\].

### Artificial intelligence fairness and data bias

AI fairness and dataset bias represent distinct but related challenges. Fairness pertains to equitable model performance across demographic groups, whereas data bias refers to representational imbalance or spurious correlations arising from data collection and annotation practices. Ensuring fairness in AI-powered biomedical imaging is critical to preventing systematic bias against vulnerable or minority populations. Bias can arise from imbalanced training datasets that underrepresent certain demographic groups, leading to reduced model accuracy and reliability in these populations. Recent studies have demonstrated the impact of such disparities. For example \[\], showed that a pneumonia detection model trained on one institution's chest radiographs exhibited markedly reduced generalization performance when applied to data from different hospitals, highlighting both fairness concerns and distribution-shift issues between development and deployment. Similar concerns have been raised regarding algorithmic performance across racial, gender, and socioeconomic subgroups \[,\], with calls for explicit subgroup performance reporting and fairness auditing in medical AI.

In a recent work \[\], researchers critically examined the limits of fair medical imaging, highlighting that while removing demographic ‘shortcuts’ in disease classification models can improve in-distribution fairness, it may reduce performance, limit out-of-distribution generalizability, and overlook cases where demographics are true causal factors, underscoring the need for nuanced, context-specific use of demographic features. Complementing this perspective \[\], provides empirical evidence of persistent bias in AI for medical imaging. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive fairness evaluation protocols, intersectional bias analysis, and continuous post-deployment auditing to ensure equitable performance.

In addition to demographic bias\, dataset drift and distribution shift—changes in data characteristics over time or across sites—can degrade model performance and exacerbate inequities. Such shifts may occur due to differences in imaging equipment, acquisition protocols, or patient population profiles between the development and deployment environments. Strategies to mitigate these risks include domain adaptation, continual learning, federated training across diverse clinical sites, and pre-deployment validation on heterogeneous datasets \[\]. Deployment of AI in biomedical imaging, therefore, requires not only robust privacy safeguards but also proactive bias detection, transparent reporting of subgroup performance, and resilience against distributional changes to ensure equitable care delivery across all patient populations.

### Integration into clinical workflows

Deploying AI imaging models in healthcare presents significant challenges. Ensuring transparency, interpretability, and explainability of AI algorithms is essential for their deployment in clinical settings \[\]. Furthermore, there is a concern that AI might dehumanize healthcare, leading patients to feel detached from their caregivers and perceived merely as data points \[\]. Moreover, effective integration of AI technologies into medicine necessitates that current and future medical trainees have an understanding of AI and be aware of its limitations. Therefore, medical schools may need to revise their curricula to educate students on new skills about how to responsibly utilize powerful and transformative digital technologies \[\]. Proper training equips healthcare professionals to competently use AI solutions as a lack of understanding could result in medical errors and liability \[\].

Additionally, the adoption of AI models into healthcare workflows requires careful alignment with existing clinical processes to ensure both technical success and clinician acceptance. A typical deployment lifecycle begins with: 1) problem identification and stakeholder engagement, where clinical needs are defined in collaboration with healthcare providers; 2) data acquisition and model development, often involving multi-institutional datasets to improve generalizability; 3) validation and regulatory approval including internal testing and external validation on diverse patient cohorts; 4) pilot deployment in a controlled clinical setting, allowing measurement of impact on workflow efficiency and patient outcomes; and 5) full-scale integration with ongoing monitoring, performance audits, and updates to address evolving data distributions and clinical requirements \[\]. From a regulatory standpoint, the development and deployment of AI-based medical imaging tools are increasingly guided by international frameworks such as the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) guiding principles for adaptive AI/ML Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) \[\]. These guidelines encourage a total product lifecycle approach, ensuring that adaptive AI systems remain safe, effective, and interpretable throughout their evolution. For clinical developers, adherence to such frameworks—alongside regional regulations—e.g., the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medical Device Regulation (MDR)—helps establish trust, accountability, and regulatory readiness for AI-driven clinical workflows.

Despite the promise of AI-enhanced workflows such as accelerated image interpretation and improved diagnostic consistency\, deployment into clinical practice faces common drawbacks. One major challenge is false alarms, which can lead to alarm fatigue among clinicians, reducing their responsiveness to alerts and potentially delaying critical interventions, and ultimately reducing their trust in the AI-powered system \[\]. This phenomenon can be exacerbated when AI models are tuned for high sensitivity without sufficient consideration for specificity. Successful adoption of AI in healthcare workflows, therefore, requires not only robust technical performance but also human-centered design principles, iterative feedback loops with end-users, clear communication of model confidence and limitations, and continuous post-deployment monitoring to mitigate false alarms and maintain clinical relevance over time.

## Conclusion

In conclusion, this high-level review has spotlighted the recent advancements in AI and computer vision for biomedical engineering and clinician-scientists, emphasizing the transformative role of AI-powered biomedical imaging. While not exhaustive, the review provides a snapshot of the technological trends and developments that are shaping the future of healthcare. It also underscores the critical considerations for integrating AI and computer vision into medical practices, highlighting both the potential benefits and challenges. As these technologies continue to evolve, it is essential for researchers and clinicians to stay informed and adapt to these changes to enhance patient care and outcomes.

### Image segmentation

Recent progress in DL has notably advanced the field of image segmentation, a process that entails the precise delineation of anatomical structures to attain optimal accuracy. Accurate segmentation is vital for clinical applications, e.g.\, disease monitoring and diagnosis. Approaches utilizing CNNs are predominant. The initial and most straightforward methods for segmentation with CNNs involved classifying individual pixels within small image patches. In 2015, the introduction of the U-Net architecture \[\] impacted medical image segmentation, paving the way for the creation of various enhanced versions of the encoder-decoder architecture to further boost its effectiveness. For instance, the nnU-Net (no new U-Net) framework, which builds upon the encoder-decoder structure of U-Net, autonomously configures pre-processing, network architecture, training, inference, and post-processing strategies tailored to any dataset \[\]. While these techniques have set new benchmarks in various medical segmentation tasks, they require significant amounts of high-quality annotated data for effective training.

To tackle the challenges of labeling in medical image segmentation, significant efforts have been directed toward creating annotation-efficient DL methods. These approaches aim to expand training data by generating labels, augmenting data, utilizing related external labeled datasets, and employing semi-supervised learning to leverage unlabeled data. Among these strategies, semi-supervised segmentation (e.g., Refs. \[,\]) is notably practical as it enables models to effectively utilize readily available unlabeled data in conjunction with a limited set of labeled data for training \[\]. However, a major limitation of many medical image segmentation models is their task-specific design, which often results in a notable decline in performance when applied to new tasks or different types of imaging data \[\]. Recently, foundation models, i.e., models that can be trained once and subsequently utilized for a broad spectrum of segmentation tasks, have been introduced to address this limitation by facilitating universal image segmentation. ### Explainability

To boost trust and acceptance of AI solutions in clinical settings, significant efforts are underway to address the black-box problem with eXplainable AI (XAI) methods, which aim to clarify the ‘hows and whys’ behind deep neural networks. Recently, several XAI techniques have been introduced to improve the interpretability of DNNs in medical imaging. These techniques generally fall into four categories: 1) Perturbation-based, 2) Gradient-based, 3) Decomposition, and 4) Content-based methods. While critics argue that models should be designed to be inherently interpretable from the outset, rather than relying on post-hoc XAI techniques to interpret black-box models \[\], these post-hoc techniques have nonetheless proven valuable in specific healthcare domains.

*Perturbation-based* methods assess feature importance by examining how changes in input affect model outputs. By modifying specific regions of an image, these methods pinpoint areas that have a significant impact on predictions. These methods are crucial for examining CNNs and understanding their behavior in safety-critical areas where transparency is essential \[\]. Various techniques within this category such as integrated gradients (IG) \[\] and local interpretable model-agnostic explanations (LIME) \[\], have been utilized in diverse medical applications, e.g., identifying congestive heart failure in chest radiographs \[\].

Backpropagation is a method for adjusting neural network weights during training. In *gradient-based* techniques, backpropagation is also used to compute gradients, where these gradients highlight important areas within an image without altering the weights \[\]. Different gradient-based techniques have been used for medical image analysis. Saliency maps \[\] utilize gradient information to elucidate the process by which deep convolutional networks classify images. The guided backpropagation method \[\] discards gradients when either the forward activation or the backward gradient is negative, thereby producing heatmaps that highlight pixels positively influencing classification. Class activation mapping (CAM) \[\] is another technique that emphasizes regions of an image that have a substantial influence on a neural network's classification decision. Additionally, Grad-CAM \[\] extends CAM's usability to various network architectures and outputs. presents an example of a Grad-CAM annotation, highlighting clinically relevant artifacts within a COVID-19-positive ultrasound image \[\].

*Decomposition-based* techniques break down a model's prediction by creating a heatmap that depicts the contribution of each pixel to the final decision. Layer-wise relevance propagation (LRP) \[\] is one of these techniques that calculates relevance scores for each neuron by distributing the output of the final layer back through the network, rather than using gradients, to create a relevancy heatmap. Other common techniques in this category include softmax-gradient layer-wise relevance propagation (SGLRP) \[\] and concept relevance propagation (CRP) \[\], to name a few.

While post-hoc XAI methods, such as Grad-CAM, have advanced our ability to visualize model reasoning, an important limitation is that these technical outputs do not always translate into clinically interpretable insights. Such visualizations often lack formal validation against clinicians' diagnostic reasoning, which can limit their utility in practice. Despite its widespread use, Grad-CAM is known to produce unstable saliency maps that may vary even under consistent model predictions and can be susceptible to adversarial perturbations, raising concerns about the reliability and robustness of visual explanations in safety-critical clinical settings. Moreover, a good medical AI explanation should be tailored to the user's expertise—while a visual disease localization may suffice for radiologists or imaging researchers, clinicians may need XAI integrated into a broader clinical decision-making framework that includes patient history, treatments, options, and expected outcomes \[\]. Clinician-in-the-loop approaches offer a promising pathway to bridge this gap. Embedding domain concepts into explanations not only improves interpretability but also facilitates trust, regulatory acceptance, and safe integration into healthcare workflows.

Moreover, beyond classic saliency-based visualizations, recent advances in XAI for medical imaging have explored methods that assess faithfulness and robustness of explanations, as well as generate counterfactual and simulatability-based interpretations. Faithfulness tests ensure that explanations accurately reflect the model's decision-making process, while robustness analyses examine stability under perturbations. Counterfactual approaches such as calibration-based counterfactuals for clinical predictions (TraCE) \[\], smooth counterfactual generation in CXR diagnosis \[\], and counterfactual image explanation using adversarial image-to-image translation techniques (GANterfactual) \[\], allow clinicians to explore how small, targeted changes in input data could alter diagnostic outcomes, thereby improving trust and actionable insight. Prototype-based XAI methods including XProtoNet \[\] and pseudo-class part-prototype networks (PCPPN) for fine-grained disease classification \[\], provide interpretable reasoning by referencing representative image regions from the training set that are most similar to the query case, offering local and global explanations. Another emerging approach is the concept bottleneck model (CBM) paradigm \[,\], which constrains the model to first predict a set of human-understandable intermediate concepts before making a final decision, thereby enabling direct clinical validation of the decision pathway. Together, these methods aim to complement traditional saliency maps by offering richer, more reliable, and clinically meaningful explanations that align with medical reasoning processes.

### Emerging artificial intelligence trends in biomedical imaging

Foundation models (FMs) represent a significant shift from traditional task-specific models as they are adaptable for various tasks beyond their initial training, having transformative potential in healthcare. FMs possess robust generative abilities and can effectively integrate diverse data modalities. This integration allows for the exploration of biomedical concepts across various scales and facilitates knowledge transfer between different data modalities \[\]. However, FMs introduce distinct challenges including the potential for over-generalization and ethical issues, necessitating meticulous implementation and ongoing oversight \[\]. Given the potential and benefits of FMs, research is actively exploring their integration with other approaches. For instance, a recent potential direction is the integration of FMs with federated learning, which provides a decentralized method for training models across multiple nodes or devices, emphasizing privacy \[\].

Vision-based medical foundation models, e.g., SAM-Med3D \[\], have tailored image-based architectures to accommodate a range of medical imaging modalities. A recent advancement to go beyond the task-specific paradigm is the development of multimodal foundation models, which integrate various data types, such as images, text, video, and audio, blending diverse architectures to achieve a comprehensive multimodal understanding \[\]. Some notable examples of these models include SurgVLP \[\] and OneLLM \[\]. This recent progress focuses on creating generalist medical AI (GMAI), an advanced type of medical foundation model designed to perform a variety of tasks with minimal or no task-specific labeled data \[\]. GMAI models have three distinct capabilities: 1) they can easily adapt to new tasks by understanding descriptions in plain language without retraining; 2) they can flexibly interact with varying combinations of data modalities for inputs and outputs, unlike rigid multimodal models; and 3) they encapsulate medical knowledge, allowing them to navigate unfamiliar tasks and employ accurate medical terminology to articulate their outputs. This adaptability enables GMAI models to remain effective in evolving environments and keep up with new diseases and technologies, avoiding the need for complete retraining \[\]. One may note that although GMAI models hold transformative potential for medical AI applications, their development and deployment are associated with significant computational complexities. Training such models often requires large clusters and distributed pipelines to handle multimodal data at scale \[\]. Moreover, inference latency remains a critical challenge for real-time clinical adoption, necessitating optimizations such as quantization, pruning, and hardware-specific acceleration \[\]. Additionally, the energy footprint of large-scale training raises sustainability concerns, highlighting the need for efficient architectures and resource-aware training strategies.

In addition to GMAI, vision-language foundation models represent a rapidly growing area in medical imaging AI. Vision-language models, leverage self- and semi-supervised learning to integrate medical imaging and textual data for tasks such as classification, segmentation, and report generation, reducing annotation needs, improving generalization, and streamlining diagnostic workflows \[\]. It is argued that foundation models in radiology can reduce data annotation bottlenecks and improve fairness and generalization by leveraging large unlabeled datasets, but they still face transparency and bias challenges, requiring decentralized clinical validation and continuous training frameworks to ensure responsible, adaptive deployment \[\].

Finally, we want to emphasize AI's potential to enhance real-time and point-of-care imaging, thus increasing accessibility. A notable example is AI in point-of-care ultrasound (POCUS), given the growing compactness and portability of ultrasound devices \[\]. Clinicians are increasingly using POCUS for specific clinical inquiries both at the bedside and in remote areas \[\], but inadequate training remains a major obstacle preventing physicians from maximizing its use \[\]. AI-powered solutions can minimize operator dependency and shorten the learning curve for acquiring POCUS skills \[\], facilitating its adoption by more clinicians. This field is still in its early stages \[\], and the effectiveness of certain applications is yet to be fully understood.

## Key challenges and issues

AI has the potential to revolutionize clinical practice and improve patient outcomes. However, several challenges need to be addressed. This section explores the main obstacles.

### Data challenges

Diverse, high-quality medical imaging datasets are required for these deep data-hungry models, which are less available in the medical field than in the general domain. Comprehensive public datasets (similar to the MIMIC \[\]) are very limited yet crucial for effectively building AI models in healthcare applications. Building and managing these datasets necessitates partnerships among multiple parties, such as researchers, healthcare practitioners, and institutions \[\].

### Privacy and ethical considerations

Accessing medical data involves navigating both technical challenges and legal and ethical complexities \[\]. The need for high-quality large-scale data for training DNNs against protecting sensitive patient data presents a multi-faceted challenge \[\]. It is essential to implement well-established data governance policies, utilize state-of-the-art anonymization techniques, and adhere to regulatory frameworks to safeguard patient privacy \[\]. In addition, in real-world clinical settings\, data is often imbalanced, featuring an uneven distribution of conditions\, diseases\, demographics, etc \[\]. It is crucial to ensure that models are developed to be both robust and free from bias. A fundamental approach to safeguarding sensitive medical information is differential privacy (DP), which introduces controlled randomness into data or model parameters to ensure that individual patient records cannot be inferred from aggregate outputs \[\]. While DP focuses on preserving confidentiality in centralized data processing, emerging decentralized approaches such as federated learning (FL) extend this principle by enabling collaborative training across institutions without sharing raw data. As an increasingly important privacy-preserving approach in biomedical imaging, FL enables collaborative model training across multiple institutions without exchanging raw patient data. In FL, models are trained locally on site-specific datasets, and only the learned parameters are shared and aggregated, thereby safeguarding sensitive information and complying with regulatory frameworks \[\]. This decentralized paradigm is particularly valuable in multi-institutional settings, where heterogeneity in imaging protocols, patient demographics, and disease prevalence can otherwise hinder model generalizability. Studies have shown that FL can enhance cross-institutional validation by reducing performance degradation when AI systems are deployed in unseen clinical environments \[\]. Nonetheless, challenges remain including communication overhead\, domain shift across participating institutions, and the need for harmonized data annotation standards. Addressing these issues is essential for enabling privacy-conscious, generalizable AI solutions capable of safe and effective real-world deployment. In summary, several legal and ethical issues need to be considered such as those related to medical liability, maintaining transparency and traceability, providing explainability, managing sensitive health data, adhering to regulatory compliance, addressing product liability, and securing malpractice insurance \[\].

### Artificial intelligence fairness and data bias

AI fairness and dataset bias represent distinct but related challenges. Fairness pertains to equitable model performance across demographic groups, whereas data bias refers to representational imbalance or spurious correlations arising from data collection and annotation practices. Ensuring fairness in AI-powered biomedical imaging is critical to preventing systematic bias against vulnerable or minority populations. Bias can arise from imbalanced training datasets that underrepresent certain demographic groups, leading to reduced model accuracy and reliability in these populations. Recent studies have demonstrated the impact of such disparities. For example \[\], showed that a pneumonia detection model trained on one institution's chest radiographs exhibited markedly reduced generalization performance when applied to data from different hospitals, highlighting both fairness concerns and distribution-shift issues between development and deployment. Similar concerns have been raised regarding algorithmic performance across racial, gender, and socioeconomic subgroups \[,\], with calls for explicit subgroup performance reporting and fairness auditing in medical AI.

In a recent work \[\], researchers critically examined the limits of fair medical imaging, highlighting that while removing demographic ‘shortcuts’ in disease classification models can improve in-distribution fairness, it may reduce performance, limit out-of-distribution generalizability, and overlook cases where demographics are true causal factors, underscoring the need for nuanced, context-specific use of demographic features. Complementing this perspective \[\], provides empirical evidence of persistent bias in AI for medical imaging. These findings underscore the need for comprehensive fairness evaluation protocols, intersectional bias analysis, and continuous post-deployment auditing to ensure equitable performance.

In addition to demographic bias\, dataset drift and distribution shift—changes in data characteristics over time or across sites—can degrade model performance and exacerbate inequities. Such shifts may occur due to differences in imaging equipment, acquisition protocols, or patient population profiles between the development and deployment environments. Strategies to mitigate these risks include domain adaptation, continual learning, federated training across diverse clinical sites, and pre-deployment validation on heterogeneous datasets \[\]. Deployment of AI in biomedical imaging, therefore, requires not only robust privacy safeguards but also proactive bias detection, transparent reporting of subgroup performance, and resilience against distributional changes to ensure equitable care delivery across all patient populations.

### Integration into clinical workflows

Deploying AI imaging models in healthcare presents significant challenges. Ensuring transparency, interpretability, and explainability of AI algorithms is essential for their deployment in clinical settings \[\]. Furthermore, there is a concern that AI might dehumanize healthcare, leading patients to feel detached from their caregivers and perceived merely as data points \[\]. Moreover, effective integration of AI technologies into medicine necessitates that current and future medical trainees have an understanding of AI and be aware of its limitations. Therefore, medical schools may need to revise their curricula to educate students on new skills about how to responsibly utilize powerful and transformative digital technologies \[\]. Proper training equips healthcare professionals to competently use AI solutions as a lack of understanding could result in medical errors and liability \[\].

Additionally, the adoption of AI models into healthcare workflows requires careful alignment with existing clinical processes to ensure both technical success and clinician acceptance. A typical deployment lifecycle begins with: 1) problem identification and stakeholder engagement, where clinical needs are defined in collaboration with healthcare providers; 2) data acquisition and model development, often involving multi-institutional datasets to improve generalizability; 3) validation and regulatory approval including internal testing and external validation on diverse patient cohorts; 4) pilot deployment in a controlled clinical setting, allowing measurement of impact on workflow efficiency and patient outcomes; and 5) full-scale integration with ongoing monitoring, performance audits, and updates to address evolving data distributions and clinical requirements \[\]. From a regulatory standpoint, the development and deployment of AI-based medical imaging tools are increasingly guided by international frameworks such as the International Medical Device Regulators Forum (IMDRF) guiding principles for adaptive AI/ML Software as a Medical Device (SaMD) \[\]. These guidelines encourage a total product lifecycle approach, ensuring that adaptive AI systems remain safe, effective, and interpretable throughout their evolution. For clinical developers, adherence to such frameworks—alongside regional regulations—e.g., the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) or the European Medical Device Regulation (MDR)—helps establish trust, accountability, and regulatory readiness for AI-driven clinical workflows.

Despite the promise of AI-enhanced workflows such as accelerated image interpretation and improved diagnostic consistency\, deployment into clinical practice faces common drawbacks. One major challenge is false alarms, which can lead to alarm fatigue among clinicians, reducing their responsiveness to alerts and potentially delaying critical interventions, and ultimately reducing their trust in the AI-powered system \[\]. This phenomenon can be exacerbated when AI models are tuned for high sensitivity without sufficient consideration for specificity. Successful adoption of AI in healthcare workflows, therefore, requires not only robust technical performance but also human-centered design principles, iterative feedback loops with end-users, clear communication of model confidence and limitations, and continuous post-deployment monitoring to mitigate false alarms and maintain clinical relevance over time.

While not exhaustive, the review provides a snapshot of the technological trends and developments that are shaping the future of healthcare. It also underscores the critical considerations for integrating AI and computer vision into medical practices, highlighting both the potential benefits and challenges. As these technologies continue to evolve, it is essential for researchers and clinicians to stay informed and adapt to these changes to enhance patient care and outcomes.
